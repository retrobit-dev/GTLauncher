# GTLauncher MatePad Mini preview

The browser preview places a responsive, non-scrollable 16:10 sky inside a landscape tablet-shaped frame. The rounded bezel and upper-left punch-hole are illustrative only; the preview does not claim physical-size or DPI accuracy. The rendered homescreen canvas has no visible text, icons, widgets, or controls. The browser adds only a weather-mode control below the tablet; it is not part of the Android homescreen.

## Dimetric projection and game look

The island uses a fixed 45° azimuth and 25° elevation above the horizon in both renderers. A 28-unit tile width projects to about 11.8 units of vertical ground depth (a 2.37:1 top-face ratio); the grid axes remain symmetric, with a screen diagonal of about 22.9°. The 9×9 grid uses logical columns A–I and rows 1–9 (row 1 at the top, row 9 at the bottom); coordinate names are internal and are not drawn over the homescreen. Before dimetric projection, columns advance left-to-right and rows top-to-bottom. The diamond compass frame is rotated 45° so the grid axes are cardinal: one row of tiles is a parallel of latitude running east–west and one column of tiles is a meridian of longitude running north–south. On each diamond tile, the top/right/bottom/left corners are northwest/northeast/southeast/southwest; the upper-right/lower-right/lower-left/upper-left edges are north/east/south/west. The camera therefore faces northwest, and device headings are shifted by 45° so sky bearings stay on real compass directions. The cardinal axes are not drawn on the surface: the grass stays free of any cross or labels, and a row of tiles still reads as a parallel of latitude while a column reads as a meridian of longitude. Reducing tile width from 36 to 28 scene units keeps the surface footprint about 252×106.5 units while adding tiles. At the illustrative 250 m per tile scale, the 9×9 patch represents 2.25 km per side. The rounded grass grid alternates Soft Fern (#A8C795) and Fern Sage (#98BA87) with reduced contrast and a soft mid-green underlay instead of dark internal seams; the 8-unit grass band sits above a continuous warm-brown soil body with a 16-unit exposed band and a 4-unit inset per side. The whole island is raised 10 scene units (bottom margin 8 to 18 units), leaving visible sky beneath the unchanged terrain slab. Camera controls remain absent from the preview. For a stronger game look, the most coherent direction is to retain this consistent dimetric grid for the island and future world props, while keeping the sky gradient, rain, fog, and lightning as atmospheric screen-space effects rather than forcing them onto the grid.

## Live sky

The preview uses the browser's current time and, when the browser grants geolocation, the device's coordinates. If geolocation is unavailable or denied, it uses Rembang, Central Java (`-6.7097, 111.3476`). Coordinates are sent to Open-Meteo to request current weather; the preview refreshes about every 30 minutes. The live label reports whether it is using the device location or the Rembang fallback and shows the last fetched cloud-cover percentage; before the first successful fetch it says the weather data has not arrived. If a request fails, it keeps the most recently received conditions for the current page session and continues rendering the sky from the local time.

Both renderers draw the same sky objects: a Sun disc with a breathing halo, a phased Moon once the Sun is down, and layered cloud sprites. The time/weather-adaptive sky gradient and island lighting remain, as do non-object weather effects such as rain, fog, and lightning. Stars are the one sky layer that stays removed.

## Model and approximations

The notes below describe how the visible sky objects are drawn: the Sun and Moon discs, their shared halo, and the cloud sprite layer.

- The old sky objects were removed from both renderers rather than just disabled: the pixel sun disc, rays, bevel, pulse glow, phased moon, star field and horizon glow are gone from the code. `sunPosition()` is retained because its altitude and azimuth still drive the sky gradient and the island lighting. The replacement sun is a plain pixel disc with no face: a full 10.5-unit circle with no rays, outline or facets, filled with a soft two-colour vertical gradient that is intentionally bright, near-white cream at the top into golden yellow at the bottom, with no quantisation steps. A halo of 14 stacked faint discs surrounds it: the lower the sun, the wider and flatter the glow (longer atmospheric path), and the higher the sun, the tighter and rounder it gets, so the glow shape follows the current latitude, longitude and solar position. The halo also breathes, with the discs sliding outward and back on a slow 1.15 rad/s cycle so it reads as alive rather than a static ring. The moon reuses the identical construction: one disc with the same two-colour vertical gradient in a cooler palette (#F4F7FB to #B9C6DA), the same stacked halo, and the same elevation-driven shape. Only the phase mask differs - the terminator is projected as an ellipse, so 100% illumination is a full disc, 50% a straight terminator through the centre, and 20% a thin crescent, driven by the real Sun-Moon elongation. The lit limb follows waxing/waning and flips for the southern hemisphere, and the moon position uses the same simplified Keplerian elements as Android's SkyMath.moon. The moon is only drawn once the sun has dropped below -2 degrees altitude, fading in across 4 degrees, so no pale crescent competes with the sun in the daytime sky. Its position follows the device clock: bodies follow a gently tilted arc that matches the diamond frame: they start slightly lower on the eastern right side and finish slightly higher on the western left side, with a shallow 9-unit tilt so the sun still sinks toward the horizon instead of setting high in the sky. The screen column comes from the body's hour angle, its progress along that arc, while altitude sets the height in the celestial band; alpha ramps linearly from altitude -1 to +3 and it is hidden below that. The palette warms near the horizon and dulls by 40% under heavy cloud. The sun draws behind the island and only within the upper half of the screen. The Sun uses a low-precision NOAA/Meeus-style apparent solar longitude and Greenwich sidereal-time conversion. Atmospheric refraction and topocentric solar parallax are omitted. The Moon uses simplified Keplerian orbital elements rather than the full lunar perturbation series and topocentric parallax. Bright-star J2000 coordinates are precessed with the Meeus/IAU 1976 model; proper motion, aberration, and nutation are omitted. The Moon's pixel-art limb, glow, warm/cool facet colors, and star twinkle remain artistic effects; its illumination-driven phase mask is unchanged. The Sun keeps its upper-edge path and glow, with a low-poly beveled disc and short rays but no face. Stars remain small astronomical pixel points.
- Clouds are raster sprites traced from the reference art rather than drawn by hand. Each of the four reference clouds was isolated against its #9FDBFF background, downsampled to a 30-column grid, and quantised into three flat light bands, so the real lobes and notches survive at this size without breaking the pixel-art language. A majority filter removes the JPEG speckle that would otherwise leave stray mid-tone cells inside a white lobe. Each sprite is also mirrored, giving eight silhouettes. Cells are a single 1-unit step — half the previous 1.45–2.10 — which keeps every cell edge on the art grid; at RENDER_SCALE 2 that is exactly 2 device pixels per cell. A fractional cell was tried first and left antialiased seams across the cloud face, and re-rounding each cell independently made the silhouette zigzag. One `cell` value drives both axes, which locks every cloud to the ratio it was traced at (1.50:1 to 2.14:1) instead of stretching it into a flat smudge; the earlier hand-drawn sprites were 3.16:1 to 4.11:1 and read as stacked pyramids. Density steps with cloud cover: at least one cloud is always drawn, so a fully clear sky still keeps a single cloud in the panel; then two at 32%, three at 58% and four at 82%. Clouds are laid out by horizontal slot only: vertical placement follows the sun, so every cloud hangs below the disc. That is what lets the light reach past them and drop their shadow on the island; when the clouds sat on fixed heights, a midday sun was above all of them and no shadow could fall at all. Clouds spread from just under the sun down over the island's back half, with a minimum span so a low sun does not stack them on one line. Each cloud travels right to left across the whole frame and re-enters from the opposite side, wrapping over the frame width plus its own width so the re-entry is seamless; a wrapped copy is drawn while a sprite straddles an edge. Travel speed comes from the reported wind (0.55 + 0.16 x km/h, clamped to 0.18-5.5 scene units per second), so a breeze crosses the panel in about five minutes and a storm in about one; a wind with a westward component reverses the travel. The oscillating sway this replaced was several times faster and never actually crossed the frame. Cells snap to the art grid so every edge stays on a whole pixel. Three flat bands follow the same light model as the Sun and Moon: day #A9C0D9/#E4EEFB/#FFFFFF, dusk #8C7F9C/#BBAEC6/#EFD7D2, night #404E6A/#5C6D8A/#8FA0BD and a separate storm palette. Clouds draw behind the Sun so a disc never disappears behind a lobe, but in front of the island: they are a foreground layer. The sky clip does not apply to them, because a foreground cloud can hang below the horizon line and clipping it there used to hide the cloud while its shadow still showed on the grass. The sky clip runs from above the frame down to the horizon rather than stopping at half height, which used to behead any cloud tall enough to reach past it. Cloud shadows are back, but only on the island top and only for clouds that actually hang over it. Each one is a full diamond plane drawn in the same flat ground-shading the island's own cast shadow uses, sized from the cloud's footprint and squashed by the same tile ratio, so the two shadow types read as one system rather than a sprite-shaped decal next to a geometric one. A cloud's shadow lands where that cloud appears in its own band, which keeps a shadow on the island for every cloud the band places there; a literal sun-ray projection would push the shadow past the island's front edge at this panel size. The plate is also shifted sideways with the light so it never sits dead centre. The sprites no longer carry range, altitude or wind advection.
- Cloud shadows are projected opposite the Sun from each cloud base onto the island's flat top plane. They retain the cloud mask silhouette; modeled footprint scaling is capped at 52% and dynamically reduced further so physical and fallback shadows stay below 90% of the cloud sprite's screen width and height. Cloud-shadow opacity was reduced to 0.10 in clear skies and a 0.20 maximum in storms; the fallback cue uses at least 0.16 times the solar daylight factor. This keeps the mask visible as a depth cue without overpowering the grass. Values remain heuristic. Above 3° solar elevation, the renderer draws physical shadows that hit the island; if every physical projection misses, a single low-opacity cue from the nearest cloud is pulled toward the island interior while preserving its direction. This cue is a stylized depth hint, not a physical projection, and does not move the cloud. The island body's cast shadow is 1.55× its base size, raised 1 scene unit and kept tucked close under the island, with a 46% opacity factor for a softer appearance. It also follows the light: the shadow drifts to the opposite side of the arc as the sun or moon travels, using that body's own progress along the arc, and it hands over to the moon once the sun is down. The 9×9 island grid represents 250 m per tile as an illustrative scale, not a surveyed location. Its logical coordinates are columns A–I and rows 1–9. The 9×9 surface uses rounded dimetric tiles with visible, softly shaded front faces and a low-contrast checkerboard pattern. Tile tops remain solid colors; when the Sun is above the horizon, each entire tile receives only a subtle, uniform Pale Moss tint, attenuated by cloud cover. No directional gradient is drawn inside grass tiles, and the Sun sprite remains hidden. The exposed sides of the perimeter tiles meet edge-to-edge without gaps through the full 8-unit grass band, preserving each tile's fern hue, and meet the inset ledge over one continuous warm-brown soil slab: the body is inset 4 scene units per side (about 97% of the grassy top width) and has no internal soil-grid seams. Alternating tile tops use Soft Fern (#A8C795) and Fern Sage (#98BA87), over a Muted Pine (#5A7365) grass base; internal seams use a subtle mid-green underlay rather than a dark outline. Pale Moss (#B6D0A1) is reserved for direct sunlight, and the tones remain adaptive to time and weather. The 24-unit side wall has an 8-unit grass band above 16 units of exposed soil. Pixel flecks and wind-swaying blades are removed to keep the island geometry-only; no crops, trees, or animals are added at this stage. The island shadow remains a stylization rather than a full lighting simulation. The old character sprite and its shadow have been removed from the renderer; the next character design will be created from scratch. The same horizontal-surface projector can still clip to future objects by footprint and elevation.
- The island palette remains adaptive to solar altitude and weather. Cloud shadow projection was removed along with the simulated cloud field, so the island shows only its own cast shadow. Liquid-rain particles still follow weather codes/data; fog and lightning remain weather effects, and precipitation can fall through the full scene. Weather remains the latest provider snapshot, so it may not be sampled at exactly the same instant as the astronomical frame. GPS accuracy, weather coverage, and sensor calibration remain approximate; no forecast accuracy beyond provider data is implied.

The button below the tablet cycles through Live (Open-Meteo), sunny day, overcast, rain, storm, and dusk/night. Demo modes only override preview visuals and synthetic sky time; clicking the control does not make another weather request, and returning to Live restores the latest fetched conditions. A browser location prompt may appear. Location is optional; denying it selects the Rembang fallback. The preview does not store coordinates or weather data beyond the current page session. It does not ask for an API key.

## Run locally

```sh
python3 -m http.server 4173 --bind 0.0.0.0 --directory preview
```

Geolocation generally requires HTTPS or localhost. The weather request is made directly from the browser to Open-Meteo.
